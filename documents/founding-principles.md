# Founding Principles and Statutory Ownership Framework

## 1. The problem

Digital distribution often separates the consumer's payment from practical control of the product. A consumer can pay in full for software described as a permanent purchase, yet remain dependent on a storefront, account, authentication server, or publisher's continued business operations. When those systems change or disappear, the consumer can lose access despite having paid for a lasting license.

The framework begins with a straightforward proposition: a permanent purchase should create a durable consumer entitlement.

## 2. Ownership of the license, not the publisher's intellectual property

The proposal recognizes a distinct statutory property interest in the consumer's qualifying purchased license entitlement. It does not transfer copyright, source code, trademarks, patents, trade secrets, or other intellectual property from the publisher.

The publisher retains its intellectual property. The consumer owns the purchased entitlement and receives the statutory rights needed to install, reinstall, use, preserve, share, lend, transfer, sell, or gift that entitlement.

This distinction is essential. Ownership of a license entitlement is not ownership of the underlying software copyright.

## 3. Storefronts administer; they do not own

A storefront or distribution platform may process payment, deliver files, authenticate an account, or maintain an entitlement record. Those administrative functions do not make the storefront the owner of the consumer's purchased license.

A storefront's closure or policy change should not erase the consumer's statutory property interest.

## 4. Rights, not permissions

The proposal treats core protections as nonwaivable statutory rights, not discretionary permissions granted by a platform. Standard-form terms may not defeat the Act.

## 5. Minimums, not maximums

The Act establishes a minimum floor. It should not reduce stronger protections already available under law, contract, or technical capability.

## 6. Immediate consumer rights; phased implementation

Rights attach on the Act's effective date, including for qualifying earlier purchases. Businesses may receive time to build systems and procedures, but implementation periods do not suspend consumer rights. Interim manual procedures should be available where feasible.

## 7. Digital and physical parity

Digital delivery should not strip fundamental installation, reinstallation, use, preservation, and transfer rights that consumers reasonably associate with a permanent purchase on physical media.

## 8. Portability and platform generations

A storefront change should not force repurchase of the same platform version. A compatible successor generation should not be treated as an entirely new platform merely because it has a new product name. Genuine technical incompatibility remains a valid distinction.

## 9. Preservation and continuity

Consumers should be able to preserve lawful personal backups and continue using software when authentication or distribution services disappear. Publishers should plan for continuity and provide reasonable preservation resources when essential online services are discontinued or materially unavailable.

## 10. Transferability

A permanent license should be transferable. Permanent transfer conveys the entitlement and requires the transferor to relinquish use. Lending is temporary and distinct. Consumers set their own resale price; a narrow fee cap applies only to a storefront's optional marketplace.

## 11. Community continuity

Where an essential online service is discontinued, consumers and communities should have the tools needed to preserve lawful functionality when technically feasible. This does not automatically require source-code disclosure or recreation of inherently remote services.

## 12. Guiding statements

- You bought it. You own the license. Your rights should follow you.
- Rights, not permissions. Ownership, not temporary access.
- Corporate failure does not erase consumer ownership.
- Software should not have an expiration date determined solely by the business decisions of its publisher.
- When a publisher closes its servers, the community should have the tools to keep the software alive.
- Ownership without the right to transfer is incomplete ownership.
