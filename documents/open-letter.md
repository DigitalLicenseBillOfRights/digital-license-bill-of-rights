# An Open Letter on Digital License Ownership

To lawmakers, software publishers, storefronts, platform companies, developers, preservationists, and consumers:

A growing share of the products people buy exists only in digital form. Consumers pay for games, applications, creative tools, educational software, and other products with the expectation that a permanent purchase means something lasting.

Yet a digital purchase can remain dependent on a storefront account, an authentication server, a publisher's continued operation, or a service that the consumer does not control. A business decision made years after the transaction can leave a paid-for product inaccessible.

That is not a durable form of ownership.

The proposed Digital License Bill of Rights starts with a clear distinction: consumers should own the permanent license entitlement they purchased, while publishers continue to own their intellectual property. The proposal does not claim that buying software transfers its copyright or source code. It says that a publisher's intellectual property rights and a consumer's purchased license entitlement can, and should, coexist.

The proposal would protect installation and reinstallation, reasonable portability, family sharing, preservation backups, lending, resale, gifting, and continued access when essential services fail. It would require reasonable continuity planning and, when essential online services are discontinued or materially unavailable, preservation resources that can help communities keep software functioning where technically feasible.

It also recognizes that business systems take time to change. Consumer rights would attach on the Act's effective date, while covered businesses could receive phased implementation deadlines for technical and administrative work. That is a transition for compliance—not a suspension of consumer rights.

The proposal is built around a simple principle: **minimums, not maximums**. It should establish a baseline without taking away stronger protections that consumers already have.

This is a working draft, not enacted law or a finished legislative instrument. It needs public scrutiny, technical review, legal analysis, and careful legislative drafting. The compliance schedule, copyright coordination, federal and state enforcement details, and other questions remain open for that work.

We invite consumers, developers, publishers, storefronts, attorneys, preservationists, and policymakers to examine the proposal, identify weaknesses, and help improve it.

A permanent purchase should not become temporary access merely because a company changes its business model or closes a server.

**You bought it. You own the license. Your rights should follow you.**
